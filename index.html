Grammar B: Expressions of Obligation, Necessity & Advice

Native speakers choose modal verbs based on Internal drive vs. External authority, Time frame, and whether an action actually happened in the past.

Internal Motivation External Rules & Laws Past Execution Context Strict Prohibition
1
English Category Keyword

Obligation and Necessity

Must / Have to / Have got to / Need to

Core Definition:

We use expressions of obligation and necessity when there is a need to do something. This need can be internal (the speaker feels it is necessary) or external (rules or the situation make it necessary).
must (mustn't)

Modal verb. Internal speaker feeling / strict law print.

"You mustn't dress too casually for work."
have to

External obligation. Rules, laws, routines.

"You have to get a work permit before you go."
have got to

Informal spoken English for immediate external need.

"I've got to find somewhere to live quite quickly."
need to

Requirement / Necessity (practical need).

"You'll need to allow a bit of extra time to get over jet lag."

Native Logic & Structural Mechanics

Invariant Form

Must is a modal verb and its form NEVER changes

Native mind-set: Pure modals do not take '-s', '-ed', or '-ing'. They sit uninflected before the base verb.

He must try a bit harder.

He musts try a bit harder.

Internal Speaker vs. External Law Origin of Obligation
Speaker Authority (Internal) → MUST

We use must when the obligation comes from the speaker's personal desire or authority.

"You must invite me to visit you." (the speaker wants this)

Institutional Rule / Law (External) → HAVE TO / NEED TO

When there is an institutional rule, law, or external necessity, have to or need to are more common than must.

"You have to get a work permit before you go." (this is an official rule)

Spoken vs. Written Register

Have to is more common in spoken English than must, but in written English either is used.

Have got to is more common in spoken English than written English:

"I've got to find somewhere to live quite quickly."

Signs, Notices & Printed Information

Must is usually chosen on public signs, official notices, and printed instructions because it sounds objective and absolute:

"All employees must hold a valid work permit."

Tense Shifts (Past & Future Obligation)

To talk about obligation and necessity in the present, we can use must(n't), have to, have got to, or need to. However, must lacks past or future tenses! We MUST switch to have to or need to for past & future:

Future Obligation

"You will have to learn some Cantonese."

You will must learn

Past Obligation

"He had to get up really early to catch the ferry to work."

He must got up early

Grammar Warning: Question Form Constraint

We do not usually make questions with must and ought to. Use need to or have to instead!

"What sort of things do you need to know?" What sort of things must you / ought you to know?

English Category Keyword

No Obligation

not have to / not need to / needn't

Core Concept:

We use not have to, not need to, and needn't to suggest that there is no obligation or necessity to do something (it is optional / unnecessary).

"You needn't buy lots of guide books before you go." (= it is not necessary to buy guide books before you go)

CRITICAL NATIVE CONTRAST: Prohibition vs. No Necessity
Option Available (No Necessity)

don't have to / don't need to / needn't

Meaning: You are free to choose. Doing it is NOT necessary, but you CAN if you want.

"You don't have to wait for ages."
(= it is not necessary to wait, but you can if you choose to)

FORBIDDEN (Prohibition)

mustn't

Meaning: You have ZERO choice. You are strictly forbidden/not allowed to do it!

"You mustn't wait here."
(= it is not allowed to wait here / strict prohibition!)

Past Lack of Obligation: Action Execution Difference

To talk about lack of obligation in the past we can use: needn't have + past participle, didn't need to, or didn't have to.

Example: "We didn't have to worry about work permits when I was there." | "I needn't have bothered to get a work permit."

didn't need to Action NOT Done

The speaker knew it was unnecessary in advance, so they DID NOT perform the action.

"John picked me up from the station so I didn't need to get a taxi home."

➔ Result: It wasn't necessary, so I did not get a taxi.

needn't have + V3 Action WAS Done (Wasted Effort)

The speaker DID perform the action, but later realized it was unnecessary.

"I needn't have got a taxi because John's flat wasn't far from the station."

➔ Result: I actually took a taxi, but it was unnecessary!

Future Lack of Obligation

To talk about the future we use not have to or not need to:

"I hope I won't have to work late tonight."
"He's not going to need to come to the meeting after all."

English Category Keyword

Suggestions and Advice

should / ought to / must
Standard Recommendation

should(n't) and ought (not) to

We can use modal verbs should(n't) and ought (not) to to make suggestions or give advice (= I think it is a good idea):

"You should try and use it whenever you can." (= I think it is a good idea)
"You ought to take lots of passport photos with you."
"You shouldn't dress casually for work."
Urgent / Enthusiastic Recommendation

must (Strong Advice)

We can use must to give strong advice or passionate recommendation:

"You must phone me when you get there."

Native nuance: It's not a military command, but an urgent, warm expression of personal care or strong endorsement!

English Category Keyword

Adverbs

Adverb Position AFTER Modal Verbs

Adverbs like also, always, never, sometimes, just and only come AFTER modal verbs:

"You should always carry plenty of loose change."
"You must never do that again."
Adding Extra Emphasis: REALLY

To add extra emphasis we can use really BEFORE the verb:

"You really have to see it to believe it."

English Category Keyword

Formal Written English

Verbs of obligation, necessity and suggestion are common in formal and academic writing when giving opinions and recommendations:

"Governments should take advice from the experts before making new laws."
"Companies need to consider cultural differences when engaging in business with overseas organisations."
